\section*{Abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Research Question/Issue:} In a Korean captive spin-off a listed parent places a business in a wholly owned subsidiary; its controlling owner later decides, without a shareholder vote, whether to keep, sell or list it, and minority shareholders lose when it is disposed of. Could that intention be read in the division filing, did the market price it, and what did the 2022 disclosure reform change?
\par\noindent\textbf{Research Findings/Insights:} Two language models read \NScored{} captive spin-off filings of 2001--2026 as of their dates. Their forecast separates subsidiaries later disposed of from those retained (AUC \AUCPooledLLM{} against \AUCPooledFin{} for the filing's financials) and survives the masking of names and dates. The announcement return rises modestly with the forecast (slope \AnnCarSlope{}, $p$ = \AnnCarSlopeP); the market-adjusted return over the following year falls with it (Spearman \SpearmanScoreBhar{}), with the loss concentrated where the disposal comes within a year and where controlling blocks are small; and the forecast falls with the block. After the reform, protection statements rose from \ProtectPre{} to \ProtectPost{} percent of filings and the announcement return from \CarPre{} to \CarPost{} percent, while the forecast and the early-disposal rate are unchanged within what a small post-reform sample can detect.
\par\noindent\textbf{Theoretical/Academic Implications:} The principal--principal literature infers the controller's intention from the expropriating outcome; here the disposal was forecastable from the controller's own disclosure beforehand, and the announcement reaction did not move against the filings that forecast it.
\par\noindent\textbf{Practitioner/Policy Implications:} The information that forecast the loss was disclosed and not priced; mandating disclosure has so far changed the filings and their reception. Protection should attach to the disposal.

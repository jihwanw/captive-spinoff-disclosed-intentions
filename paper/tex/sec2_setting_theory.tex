\section{Setting and theory}\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{Captive spin-offs in Korea}\label{sec:captive}

Korean company law allows a listed company to divide itself in two ways. In an equity spin-off (\emph{injŏk bunhal}) the new company's shares are distributed to the parent's shareholders in proportion to their holdings, as in a US spin-off; in a captive spin-off (\emph{mulchŏk bunhal}) the parent itself receives all of the new company's shares, so that the parent's shareholders now own the carved-out business only through the parent \cite{kang2025}. The second form has dominated: of the \KLKrxTotal{} division announcements the Korea Exchange counted from 1998, when the Commercial Act first allowed divisions, to 2022, \KLKrxCaptive{} were captive \cite{kang2025}; \citeA{kang2025a} analyse the choice between the two forms and their announcement effects. The form has no immediate effect on the consolidated statements and none on the parent's ownership structure. Its consequences arrive later, when the parent decides what to do with the subsidiary it now wholly owns. It may keep it, sell part or all of it, or list it, and each of these decisions is taken by the parent's board, which the controlling owner appoints, without a vote of the parent's shareholders.

The controversy of 2020--2022 concerned the disposal rather than the division. When LG Chem announced in September 2020 that it would carve out its battery business into a wholly owned subsidiary, its filing gave operational efficiency, specialisation, the subsidiary's competitiveness and its long-term growth as the purposes. The shareholders' meeting approved the division on 30 October 2020 over the opposition of some large outside investors; the subsidiary was established on 1 December 2020 as LG Energy Solution and listed on 27 January 2022, by which date LG Chem's stock had lost more than \KLLGDrop{} percent of its value since the announcement \cite{kang2025}. In our data LG Chem's filing carries a disposal signal of \LGScore{} on a scale from zero to one; the stock's three-day announcement return was \LGAnnCar{} percent and its market-adjusted return over the following year \LGBhar{} percent. By February 2022 the practice was being described in the English-language press as one that leaves the parent's shareholders ``stuck with the parent company's stock which inevitably sees its value shrink, rather than being given a stake in the more lucrative new entity'' \cite{park2022}, and by the middle of the year the protection of general shareholders in captive spin-offs was on the new administration's list of national policy tasks, on which the Financial Services Commission consulted experts and stakeholders for several months before it acted \cite{fsc2022a}. The objection has two parts that matter for what follows. The first is that the loss to the parent's minority shareholders occurs at the disposal, not at the division. The second is that the controlling owner knows, at the division, more about the disposal than the filing lets the public know.

The regulator answered in 2022. On 5 September 2022 the Financial Services Commission announced three measures for captive spin-offs \cite{fsc2022a}. First, the material-event report that announces the division must state the division's specific purpose (restructuring, sale or listing, for example), its expected effects and the measures the company will take to protect its shareholders, and must disclose and keep current any plan to list the new company. Second, shareholders who vote against a captive spin-off receive an appraisal right to sell their shares to the company at a price based on the market price before the division was pursued.\footnote{The price is the arithmetic mean of the volume-weighted average prices over the two months, the month and the week before the board's resolution, the formula of Article 165-5(3) of the Capital Markets Act, and a dissenting shareholder who disputes it may ask a court to set the price \cite{fsc2022a}.} Third, when a carved-out subsidiary applies for listing within five years of the division, the Korea Exchange reviews how the parent protected its general shareholders and may refuse the listing. The listing review applied from 28 September 2022 and the revised disclosure form from 18 October 2022. The appraisal right required an amendment to the enforcement decree of the Capital Markets Act, which the Cabinet approved on 20 December 2022 for promulgation and immediate effect before the end of the year \cite{fsc2022b}. Of the captive spin-offs in our data announced from 2017 to the September 2022 package, none states shareholder-protection measures; the first that does is dated 23 September 2022, between the package and the revised form, and \ProtectPost{} percent of the filings received from October 2022 onward do.

Two features of this setting make it useful. The disposal is a discrete event that the parent must report in its annual report, so the controlling owner's revealed intention can be measured for every division, years later, from public filings; and the filing that announces the division is a long, structured document, so what a reader could have inferred at the announcement can be measured as well.

\subsection{Controlling owners and the disposal decision}\label{sec:theory}

We read the captive spin-off through the principal--principal frame, in which the governance problem of concentrated-ownership firms is the conflict between controlling and minority owners rather than between owners and managers \cite{young2008}. The frame has a specific prediction for Korea. Controlling families hold the parent through pyramids and cross-holdings, so their cash-flow stake in a listed parent is often far below their control stake \cite{laporta1999, claessens2000}. A transfer of value out of the parent---tunneling, in the term of \citeA{johnson2000}---then costs the family only its cash-flow share of the loss, while the gain, if it accrues elsewhere in the group or to the family directly, is theirs in full, and firm value falls as the wedge between control and cash-flow rights widens \cite{claessens2002, masulis2011}. Korean business groups have been the canonical setting for documenting such transfers. \citeA{bae2002} found that when a chaebol firm acquired another firm, the acquiring firm's minority shareholders lost while the controlling family gained through its holdings in other group firms; \citeA{baek2006} found private securities offerings within groups priced in the family's favour; \citeA{bae2008} found groups propping up members in trouble; and \citeA{joh2003}, \citeA{mitton2002} and \citeA{black2006} found lower profitability, larger crisis losses and lower market values where the controller's cash-flow stake was small or governance weak. The structure of the groups themselves is shaped by the controller's position in the pyramid \cite{almeida2011}, and the same arithmetic appears in intercorporate loans in China \cite{jiang2010} and in the premia paid for control blocks across countries \cite{dyck2004}. The most recent Korean evidence in this journal is of the same kind: when a controlling family incurs an inheritance or gift tax, its firms raise dividends, and more so where the potential for expropriation is high \cite{na2025}. The reviews of this literature make the same point in general form: where the controller's cash-flow stake is small, the wedge between control and ownership is the governance variable to watch \cite{young2008, castaner2022, aguilera2025}.

A captive spin-off is a transfer waiting to happen. The division itself moves nothing; it changes the set of decisions the controller can take without the minority, who afterwards receive their share of any sale, investment or listing proceeds only insofar as the parent's value reflects them. \citeA{kang2025} document, for \KLN{} captive spin-offs announced between 2001 and 2021, that the parent's stock earns a small positive return at the announcement and a large negative abnormal return over the following year, that the loss is concentrated where the controlling owner's stake is small, and that it is concentrated in the divisions whose subsidiary was eventually disposed of. They also write, without testing it, that investors are unlikely to discern the controller's intention at the announcement. We take that sentence as a testable claim about the filing, about the controller's stake, about the market, and about the reform that followed.

The view against which our first hypothesis is set holds that the division filing is boilerplate, written to a form, so that a careful reading predicts the disposal no better than the parent's financial position does. We expect the opposite (H1): a reading of the filing text predicts whether the subsidiary is later disposed of, and does so better than the parent's and the new company's financial characteristics at the time of the division. The reason is not that controllers confess but that the filing has an audience beyond the shareholders who vote on it. A controller who intends to list or sell the subsidiary has commercial reasons to describe the new company as a vehicle for outside capital, to mention a listing application, or to state attracting investment as a purpose, because the investors and buyers the controller will need read the same document; Doosan's 2021 filing, quoted in Section~\ref{sec:gate1}, went as far as naming the buyer. Intentions shape the stated purpose, the description of the new company and the choice of what to leave out. The hypothesis has a limit: the filing can only be informative about intentions that exist at the announcement, and disposals decided years later for reasons that arose later are noise against which any reader, human or machine, will fail.

If divisions were decided on the subsidiary's merits---the efficiency view of divestitures, in which the division is a refocusing or financing device \cite{krishnaswami1999, slovin1995, powers2003, vijh2002}---the controller's stake would have nothing to do with the disposal. The principal--principal arithmetic says otherwise. The controller's gain from a disposal (listing proceeds that fund the group, a sale to a related company, a stake sold to an investor on terms the controller sets) does not depend on the controller's cash-flow stake in the parent, while the controller's share of the parent's loss does, so a smaller stake lowers the controller's private cost of a disposal and raises the probability that the division is undertaken with one in view. We expect (H2) that the smaller the controlling block before the announcement, the stronger the disposal signal in the filing and the more likely the subsidiary is disposed of, particularly soon after the division. The stake is one determinant among several (a controller with a large stake may still dispose because the subsidiary needs capital the parent cannot supply, and a controller with a small stake may retain because the subsidiary is the group's core), so the prediction concerns the direction of an association. \citeA{allen1998}, who find that US carve-outs by parents that keep the proceeds are received worse than those that pay them out, supply the version of the efficiency view in which the market reads the parent's intentions at the announcement, and that reading is what the third hypothesis tests.

The third hypothesis concerns the market, and it is the one on which \citeA{kang2025} and the efficient-markets view make opposite predictions, so we state both. If minority investors read the filing as a careful reader would, a filing that signals a disposal should be met with a weaker, or more negative, announcement return than one that signals retention, and nothing systematic should follow: the loss from the disposal would be in the price at the announcement, and the subsequent return would be unrelated to the signal. If, as \citeA{kang2025} and the practitioners assert, investors do not discern the intention, the announcement return should be unrelated to the signal---or positively related to it, if a filing that describes outside capital and a listing reads as a growth story---and the loss should arrive afterwards as the disposal materialises, so that the subsequent return falls with the signal and with the realised disposal, most steeply where the disposal comes quickly and where the controlling block is small. We state H3 as the efficient-reading prediction (a more negative announcement return and no subsequent drift where the filing signals disposal) and treat the second pattern as evidence for the rival. The test requires the signal to be measurable at the announcement, which H1 supplies, and a one-year window that captures the disposals that follow quickly.

The fourth hypothesis concerns the reform, and the view against which it is set is the one the regulator's own description implies: that mandated disclosure, an appraisal right and a listing review deter disposal-oriented divisions, so that the disposal signal in post-reform filings is weaker and realised early disposal rarer. We expect (H4) that over the horizon we can observe the reform acts on what is written, and on the price reaction to what is written, before it acts on what is done: filings state protection measures and listing intentions where they did not before, the announcement reaction changes, and the disposal signal and the rate of early disposal do not. The timing of the measures bounds the test. The appraisal right took effect in late December 2022, so the first post-reform quarter had the new form without the new right, and disposals take a year or more to be observed, so the post-reform disposal record is short.

These are predictions about associations in public records. We do not have an instrument for the controlling block or for the division decision, and we do not claim that the block causes the disposal or the return. We claim that the pattern of associations is the one the principal--principal frame predicts and not the one the efficiency view predicts, and we say where the data cannot tell the two apart.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Theory, predictions and results}\label{sec:mapping}

Table~\ref{tab:mapping} sets each hypothesis beside the prediction it made in our variables, the test, the result and our reading of it. Two readings in the table need a word beyond what Section~\ref{sec:results} gives. For H2, the block predicts the signal, and once the signal is in the regression the block adds nothing to the prediction of early disposal, which is what we would expect if the stake works by shaping the intention that the filing discloses. For H4, captive spin-offs also became rarer after the reform, a change we record without attributing it.

Two of the readings deserve the qualification the table gives them. The association between the controlling block and the realised disposal is clear in the tercile sort (\LowBlockDisp{} against \HighBlockDisp{} percent) and imprecise in the regression with controls (\BlockOnDisposed{}, standard error \BlockOnDisposedSE). What is precise is the block's association with the signal and with the return. The reform result is a before-and-after comparison with \NPostFilings{} post-reform filings and a short outcome record. It establishes that the disposal signal and the early-disposal rate stayed where they were, within the precision of the comparison, over the horizon we observe (\NPostFilings{} read filings; \NPostOutcomes{} post-reform divisions with two years of reports), not that the appraisal right and the listing review will not change behaviour once they have been exercised against a listing.

\subsection{Theoretical implications}\label{sec:theoretical}

The principal--principal literature has treated the controlling owner's intention as unobservable and has inferred it from outcomes: a merger that moves value toward the family's firms, an offering priced below market, a loan to a weak affiliate \cite{bertrand2002, bae2002, baek2006, buchuk2014}. The inference arrives only with the outcome, which is the expropriation itself. Our results say that for one class of transactions the outcome was forecastable before it occurred from the document the controller was legally required to produce to obtain the minority's vote; we read the forecast as a reading of the controller's intention, and the realised disposals are what validate it, since intention itself is not observed. A disposal-oriented division has to be described differently from a retention-oriented one if the description is to serve the controller's later purposes, and that difference is what the forecast picks up. This moves the question the literature asks about disclosure. The question has been whether more disclosure deters expropriation \cite{he2025}. The prior question is whether the disclosure already produced contains the expropriation's forecast, and whether anyone reads it. In our setting the disclosure contained the forecast, and the announcement reaction did not move against it; the price did not carry the information the filing contained.

The result also qualifies the efficient-markets presumption that attaches to event studies of divestitures. The carve-out literature in the United States reads a positive announcement return as the market's assessment of the restructuring's value \cite{schipper1986, nanda1991, slovin1995, hulburt2002}. Read that way, the Korean announcement returns in Table~\ref{tab:returns_by_score} would say that the market welcomed the divisions most likely to end in a disposal; the following year says otherwise. In a market in which the controlling block is large and the disposal is at the controller's discretion, the announcement return measures what minority investors understood, and what they understood, on this evidence, was not the disposal the filing's details implied. The forecast is extractable by a reader with no stake in the company and no knowledge of its later history, and it was not extracted by the investors who did have a stake---a fact about attention and expertise in minority ownership, of the kind \citeA{anand2026} document when retail investors' governance proposals respond to the readability of the 10-K, that the principal--principal frame has not had to confront because it has not previously had a measure of the intention separate from the outcome.

The controlling block's role is the one \citeA{young2008} and \citeA{aguilera2025} describe, but the chain we trace is specific: the block does not move the announcement reaction and does not, once the signal is in the regression, move the probability of early disposal; it moves the signal, and the signal predicts the disposal. A theory of controlling-owner behaviour that wants to predict expropriation from ownership structure can, for transactions that are announced before they are completed, add the announcement to its data.

\subsection{Practitioner implications}\label{sec:practitioner}

For the minority investor, the information needed to avoid the typical loss was in the filing, and extracting it required reading the filing for what it implied about the new company's future ownership rather than for its stated purpose. A reader who ranked filings by the signal we measure would have placed in the top quintile a group of parents whose median one-year market-adjusted return was \QFiveMedian{} percent, against \QOneMedian{} percent in the bottom quintile. A reader who took the announcement reaction as the market's verdict would have been reassured by the top quintile's reception and wrong a year later. The signal is strongest for the disposals that follow within a year, which is also where the loss is, and it reads the filing's description of the new company's future ownership rather than its stated purpose, which the post-reform filings show can be made uniform by a form.

For boards and controlling owners the finding is that the filing is read, if not by the market then by anyone who later wishes to show what the controller knew; from October 2022 the exchange will read it when the subsidiary applies to list. A controller who intends to dispose of the subsidiary and writes a retention-oriented filing creates a record that the listing review can hold against the parent; on our evidence, which on this point is a pooled slope of modest size, the announcement reaction has not penalised candour. Post-reform filings state protection measures and listing plans at rates (\ProtectPost{} and \ListingPost{} percent) that leave the statements with little power to distinguish one filing from another; we would not expect that to last once the listing review has been exercised against a parent whose statements were not honoured.

Institutional investors and proxy advisers vote on the division and not on the disposal, so the division vote is the only point at which the disposal can be contested and the filing the only document on which to contest it; the dimension our measure captures, the implied future ownership of the new company, is the one on which engagement before the vote would have to turn.

\subsection{Policy implications}\label{sec:policy}

The disclosure measure of the 2022 reform rests on the premise that general shareholders lacked the information to decide. In the Commission's own description, the mandated statements are to give them sufficient information for the vote, the appraisal right and their investment decisions \cite{fsc2022b}. Our evidence says that the information that mattered was already in the filing and was not priced, so the disclosure measure addressed a problem that was not the binding one. The measures that act on the mechanism we document are the appraisal right, which makes the division costly if minority holders dissent in numbers, and the listing review, which can refuse the disposal's most valuable form; their effects cannot yet be seen in the data. Disclosure rules can move shareholder behaviour when they change what shareholders can see about each other (the Japanese requirement to disclose institutions' voting records raised dissent in director elections; \citeNP{uchida2025}), but the 2022 statements are addressed to shareholders who, on our evidence, were not reading the filing to begin with. The practical reading for the Financial Services Commission and the Korea Exchange is that the mandated statements should be audited against subsequent conduct: a protection statement in a filing whose subsidiary applies to list within the five years the review covers should be compared with what the parent did, and the comparison published. A disclosure requirement that produces statements in \ProtectPost{} percent of filings, while the behaviour disclosed continued as before within the precision of a small sample, has so far been tested only on what the filings say.

The appraisal right must be exercised at the division, at a price based on the market before the division was pursued. At that moment the market's reaction is positive, so a shareholder who takes the price as the verdict has no reason to dissent, and the loss we document arrives over the following year as the disposal materialises. The right as designed therefore protects a shareholder who has read the filing closely enough to dissent against the market's reception of it. A right exercisable at the disposal rather than at the division, or a requirement that the parent's shareholders vote on a disposal of the subsidiary within some years of a captive spin-off, would act at the step where the loss occurs. \citeA{kang2025} reach the same recommendation from the outcome evidence; the forecastability we add implies that such a vote would be informed, and the same reasoning applies to other reorganisations with a deferred, discretionary second step, such as a transfer of a business to an affiliate ahead of its sale.

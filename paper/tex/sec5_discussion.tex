\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Theory, predictions and results}\label{sec:mapping}

Table~\ref{tab:mapping} sets each hypothesis beside the theory that produced it, the prediction in our variables, the test, the result and our reading. Two readings need a word beyond Section~\ref{sec:results}. For H2, the block predicts the signal, and once the signal is in the regression the block adds nothing to the prediction of early disposal, which is what we would expect if the stake works by shaping the intention the filing discloses. For H4, captive spin-offs also became rarer after the reform, a change we record without attributing it.

Two of the readings deserve the qualification the table gives them. The association between the controlling block and the realised disposal is clear in the tercile sort (\LowBlockDisp{} against \HighBlockDisp{} percent) and imprecise in the regression with controls (\BlockOnDisposed{}, standard error \BlockOnDisposedSE); what is precise is the block's association with the signal and with the return. The reform result is a before-and-after comparison with \NPostFilings{} post-reform filings and a short outcome record. It establishes that the disposal signal and the early-disposal rate stayed where they were, within the precision of the comparison, over the horizon we observe (\NPostFilings{} read filings; \NPostOutcomes{} post-reform divisions with two years of reports), not that the appraisal right and the listing review will not change behaviour once they have been exercised against a listing.

\subsection{Theoretical implications}\label{sec:theoretical}

The principal--principal literature has treated the controlling owner's intention as unobservable and has inferred it from outcomes: a merger that moves value toward the family's firms, an offering priced below market, a loan to a weak affiliate \cite{bertrand2002, bae2002, baek2006, buchuk2014}. The inference arrives only with the outcome, which is the expropriation itself. Our results say that for one class of transactions the outcome was forecastable before it occurred from the document the controller was legally required to produce to obtain the minority's vote; we read the forecast as a reading of the controller's intention, and the realised disposals validate it, since intention itself is not observed. The information-asymmetry theory of divestitures explains why: a disposal-oriented division has to be described to the counterparties it needs, and that description is what the forecast picks up. This moves the question the literature asks about disclosure. It has been whether more disclosure deters expropriation \cite{he2025}; the prior question is whether the disclosure already produced contains the expropriation's forecast, and whether anyone reads it. In our setting the disclosure contained the forecast and the announcement reaction did not move against it.

The result also qualifies the efficient-markets presumption that attaches to event studies of divestitures. The carve-out literature in the United States reads a positive announcement return as the market's assessment of the restructuring's value \cite{schipper1986, nanda1991, slovin1995, hulburt2002}. Read that way, the Korean announcement returns in Table~\ref{tab:returns_by_score} would say that the market welcomed the divisions most likely to end in a disposal; the following year says otherwise. In a market in which the controlling block is large and the disposal is at the controller's discretion, the announcement return measures what minority investors understood, and what they understood, on this evidence, was not the disposal the filing's details implied. The forecast is extractable by a reader with no stake in the company and no knowledge of its later history, and it was not extracted by the investors who did have a stake---a fact about attention and expertise in minority ownership, of the kind \citeA{anand2026} document when retail investors' governance proposals respond to the readability of the 10-K, that the principal--principal frame has not had to confront because it has not previously had a measure of the intention separate from the outcome.

The controlling block's role is the one \citeA{young2008} and \citeA{aguilera2025} describe, but the chain we trace is specific: the block does not move the announcement reaction and does not, once the signal is in the regression, move the probability of early disposal; it moves the signal, and the signal predicts the disposal. A theory of controlling-owner behaviour that wants to predict expropriation from ownership structure can, for transactions that are announced before they are completed, add the announcement to its data.

\subsection{Strategic implications for institutional investors}\label{sec:practitioner}

For a chief investment officer whose portfolio holds Korean parents, a captive spin-off announcement is a dated event with a measurable exposure. Moving from a filing at the retention end of the signal to one at the disposal end places the parent \RankSpecThreePctl{} percentiles lower in its announcement year's return ranking after size, value, momentum and filing controls (Table~\ref{tab:controls}); the top quintile of the signal earned a median one-year market-adjusted return of \QFiveMedian{} percent against \QOneMedian{} percent in the bottom quintile (Table~\ref{tab:returns_by_score}). The exposure is concentrated in the filings that describe the new company as a vehicle for outside capital, in the parents whose controlling block is below about \LowBlockMax{} percent of common shares (median one-year return \LowBlockMedian{} against \HighBlockMedian{} percent in the top tercile, Table~\ref{tab:block_terciles}), and in the first year after the division. A holding in such a parent is, after the announcement, a position whose relative return depends on a decision the controller has not announced but has, on this evidence, begun to reveal.

The process implication follows from the timing of the rights the investor holds. Shareholders vote on the division and not on the disposal, and the appraisal right created in 2022 exists only for those who dissent at that vote; afterwards the disposal is the board's alone. The division filing is therefore the one document, and the general meeting the one occasion, on which the disposal can be contested, and the dimension on which to contest it is whether the filing implies that the new company will remain wholly owned. That reading does not require a language model: the sentences the models quote in Section~\ref{sec:gate1}---a named buyer, a stated plan to attract outside investment, a charter clause requiring a shareholder vote before any listing---are sentences an analyst can find in an afternoon, and a stewardship policy for Korean holdings can require that finding before the vote, together with a stated position on the appraisal right. The signal orders the subsidiaries disposed of within a year against those retained with an AUC of \AUCEarlyPooled{}, so the filings worth the afternoon are identifiable.

The risk-management implication concerns what not to read. The three-day announcement return rises with the disposal signal by \AnnCarSlopePP{} percentage points over its range, so a desk that takes the market's first reaction as a verdict on the division will be reassured by exactly the filings that go on to underperform. The loss arrives with the disposal rather than with the announcement; the horizon over which a high-signal position should be reviewed is the year in which an early disposal would appear in the parent's half-year and annual reports, and the event to watch for is a change in the subsidiary's ownership, not a price move.

For boards and controlling owners the finding is that the filing is read, if not by the market then by the exchange when the subsidiary applies to list and by anyone who later wishes to show what the controller knew. A controller who intends to dispose of the subsidiary and writes a retention-oriented filing creates a record that the listing review can hold against the parent; post-reform filings state protection measures and listing plans at rates (\ProtectPost{} and \ListingPost{} percent) that leave the statements with little power to distinguish one filing from another, and we would not expect that to last once the listing review has been exercised against a parent whose statements were not honoured.

\subsection{Policy implications}\label{sec:policy}

The disclosure measure of the 2022 reform rests on the premise that general shareholders lacked the information to decide. In the Commission's own description, the mandated statements are to give them sufficient information for the vote, the appraisal right and their investment decisions \cite{fsc2022b}. Our evidence says that the information that mattered was already in the filing and was not priced, so the disclosure measure addressed a problem that was not the binding one. The measures that act on the mechanism we document are the appraisal right, which makes the division costly if minority holders dissent in numbers, and the listing review, which can refuse the disposal's most valuable form; their effects cannot yet be seen in the data. Disclosure rules can move shareholder behaviour when they change what shareholders can see about each other (the Japanese disclosure of institutions' voting records raised dissent in director elections; \citeNP{uchida2025}), but the 2022 statements are addressed to shareholders who, on our evidence, were not reading the filing. The practical reading for the Financial Services Commission and the Korea Exchange is that the mandated statements should be audited against conduct: a protection statement in a filing whose subsidiary applies to list within the five years the review covers should be compared with what the parent did, and the comparison published. A disclosure requirement that produces statements in \ProtectPost{} percent of filings, while the behaviour disclosed continued as before within the precision of a small sample, has so far been tested only on what the filings say.

The appraisal right must be exercised at the division, at a price based on the market before the division was pursued; at that moment the market's reaction is positive, so a shareholder who takes the price as the verdict has no reason to dissent, and the loss we document arrives over the following year as the disposal materialises. The right as designed protects a shareholder who has read the filing closely enough to dissent against the market's reception of it. A right exercisable at the disposal, or a requirement that the parent's shareholders vote on a disposal of the subsidiary within some years of a captive spin-off, would act at the step where the loss occurs. \citeA{kang2025} reach the same recommendation from the outcome evidence; the forecastability we add implies that such a vote would be informed, and the same reasoning applies to other reorganisations with a deferred, discretionary second step, such as a transfer of a business to an affiliate ahead of its sale.
